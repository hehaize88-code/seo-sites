Why Does PikoBuy Charge Shipping Twice?

Domestic delivery is the China leg between the seller and the warehouse. It deserves its own evidence trail because it can change before receipt, appear once for a group of items, or be confused with the international parcel charge paid later.

PikoBuy domestic shipping ends at warehouse receipt

For a purchasing order, the domestic leg begins when the seller dispatches the selected item inside China and ends when the PikoBuy warehouse receives it. The product price buys the item. Seller-to-warehouse delivery moves it to storage. International shipping begins only after approved warehouse items are packed into a parcel, a route is chosen and that parcel charge is paid.

PikoBuy’s current beginner guide presents these events as separate stages: choose specifications, colour and size; submit and pay the purchasing order; wait for warehouse inspection; then choose a shipping route and pay international shipping. That sequence is the reason a domestic delivery line should never be merged into a later freight estimate merely because both contain the word “shipping.”

Use one definition throughout your records. “Domestic” means seller or sender to warehouse. “International” means warehouse parcel to destination. A warehouse return to the seller is another domestic movement, but it belongs to an after-sales scenario rather than the original inbound line.

Follow evidence from the source page to the warehouse event

The earliest domestic amount may appear on the selected source listing or purchasing-order summary. Treat it as current evidence, not a permanent platform rate. The amount can depend on the seller, delivery terms, address, quantity or how the seller groups products. Save the selected product state and the order record together so you know what the charge relates to.

Then follow four checkpoints: order submitted, seller dispatch recorded, domestic tracking or handoff evidence available, and warehouse receipt. PikoBuy’s guide says the warehouse performs check-in, photo confirmation and a defect check after arrival. The warehouse event therefore confirms that something reached the facility; it does not automatically prove that every item, option or charge in a multi-item order was correct.

Give every seller-to-warehouse charge its own ledger row

Create a domestic-shipping ledger before the items arrive. Use columns for order ID, seller, item IDs, source platform, quantity, recorded domestic amount, evidence location, status and last checked date. Keep the original currency. If you need a planning currency, store the conversion and date in separate columns so a currency movement is not mistaken for a delivery change.

Domestic acquisition subtotal = selected product amounts + confirmed inbound domestic charges + approved order adjustments

This subtotal stops at the warehouse. Do not add packaging, international route estimates, destination taxes or return costs. Label each domestic row as confirmed , provisional , included , cash on delivery , or unresolved only when the current evidence supports that label. A blank amount is unknown; it is not automatically free.

Record adjustments as new rows. If the purchasing record changes after the seller confirms availability or scope, do not overwrite the original amount without explanation. A dated adjustment makes the final reconciliation understandable and prevents an order-stage difference from being blamed on international freight.

Reconcile seller groups before allocating cost to individual items

One seller shipment may contain several items, while several sellers normally create separate domestic movements. Start by matching the charge to the seller shipment, not by dividing every amount across every product in the account. A group-level charge should remain attached to that group until you need a per-item analysis.

For internal budgeting, choose and document an allocation rule. Equal division is simple when units are similar. Quantity share can work when identical units travel together. Product-value share may be useful for an acquisition-cost comparison, but it does not claim the seller calculated shipping that way. Keep both the original group charge and the editorial allocation so the platform record remains intact.

Do not double count. If two item rows point to one seller charge, mark the line once at group level and let the item rows reference it. If one seller dispatch is split into two domestic packages, keep two tracking or receipt events but verify whether the paid domestic charge changed before adding a second cost line.

Forwarded goods need a sender-to-warehouse record

PikoBuy’s current Shipping Terms describe a forwarding workflow that is distinct from a normal purchasing order: obtain the warehouse address, give it to the sender, submit the forwarding form, wait for receipt and photos, then submit the goods as a parcel. In this case, your domestic ledger starts with the sender and the forwarding form rather than a PikoBuy purchasing order.

The same terms say that if a forwarded parcel arrives with shipping fee payable on delivery, PikoBuy can deduct that amount from the account balance without separate confirmation. This is a specific cash-on-delivery forwarding condition; do not generalize it to every marketplace order. Record the sender’s reference, forwarding form, expected package count and account deduction evidence.

The terms also tell users to contact the sender if goods are missing on warehouse receipt and state that PikoBuy does not provide after-sales service for forwarded products, although it can help ship goods back. That makes sender identity and packing evidence especially important for this workflow.

Worked example: two sellers, three items and one grouped charge

Assume Item A and Item B come from Seller One, while Item C comes from Seller Two. The paid order shows one inbound domestic line for A and B together and a separate line for C. Do not copy Seller One’s group amount into both item rows; that would count the same charge twice.

  1. Create Group S1 containing A and B, with its single domestic line and order evidence.
  2. Create Group S2 containing C, with its separate domestic line.
  3. Keep both charges in the original currency and mark whether each is confirmed or provisional.
  4. When Seller One dispatches two packages, add two receipt checkpoints under Group S1 without inventing a second charge.
  5. When A arrives first, mark A received and B in transit; do not mark the whole group complete.
  6. After B arrives, reconcile the group’s package count, item identity and recorded domestic amount.
  7. If a per-item budget is needed, allocate the S1 amount once using a documented rule while retaining the original group total.

The calculation is structural: total inbound domestic cost equals the confirmed S1 group line plus the confirmed S2 line. The example intentionally uses no fabricated currency amount or fixed PikoBuy tariff. Your live order records supply the values.

Close the domestic ledger only after warehouse review

Arrival is the right moment to compare expected seller, item, variant, quantity and package group with the warehouse record. If one item is missing, avoid assuming the warehouse lost it. Check whether the seller split the dispatch, whether another package is still moving and whether the order scope changed. For forwarded goods, the published terms specifically direct missing-item verification to the sender.

If the received item has a seller-caused problem, keep the original inbound charge and any potential return movement separate. PikoBuy’s current Returns & Exchanges page says responsibility and costs depend on the reason, seller agreement, item condition and eligibility. For eligible customer-choice returns, the published terms can include the seller’s original shipping and shipping back to the seller; seller-caused cases are handled differently. Verify the live case rather than forecasting a universal deduction.

Close a row when the correct item has been received, its domestic amount is reconciled, and no unresolved package remains. The next cost stage is warehouse and parcel planning—not a continuation of the seller’s domestic line.
