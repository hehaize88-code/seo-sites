Use the HooBuy shipping calculator with your destination, product category, parcel weight and dimensions. Treat its result as a dated estimate for those inputs. Before paying for international shipping, compare the available route and the current parcel record again.

A product price does not tell you what a parcel will cost to deliver. A light but bulky box can behave differently from a compact parcel, and an estimate made before warehouse arrival may use dimensions that later change. This guide shows how to build a useful estimate, record uncertainty and compare packing choices without inventing a universal shipping rate.

Collect the inputs before comparing routes

The official estimator asks for destination, weight in grams, category and dimensions in centimetres. Start a separate estimate record for each destination and parcel configuration. If you change the contents or packaging, save a new version rather than overwriting the earlier assumptions.

Keep unknown inputs visible. If the item has not reached the warehouse, write “estimated” beside the weight. If you only know a product's retail-box dimensions, do not label them as the finished parcel dimensions. The label on each number matters as much as the number itself.

Convert units before entering weight

One kilogram is 1,000 grams. An estimated parcel weighing 2.4 kg should therefore be entered as 2,400 g in a grams field. Measure all three sides using the same length unit; do not combine centimetres with inches. Round only as required by the route or input field, and preserve the original measurement in your notes.

A useful record has separate columns for individual item weight, packaging allowance and packed parcel weight. Adding item weights can support early planning, but it is not a substitute for weighing the packed parcel. Do not add packaging a second time if a warehouse figure already includes it.

Understand actual and volumetric weight

Actual weight is the physical mass of a parcel. Volumetric weight describes the space it occupies using a route-specific divisor. For a route that uses this method, a larger box can increase the relevant weight even when the goods themselves are light. The divisor and billing rules must come from the route being compared.

The estimator explains length × width × height ÷ 6,000 as a general example. That does not establish 6,000 as the divisor for every shipping line. The tool below is an arithmetic helper: enter the applicable divisor yourself and compare the result with actual weight. It does not retrieve rates or determine route eligibility.

Follow a worked parcel example

Consider an illustrative parcel measuring 40 × 30 × 25 cm and weighing 2,400 g. With an assumed divisor of 6,000, its calculated volumetric weight is 5 kg. If the selected route charges the greater of actual and volumetric weight, 5 kg would be the starting weight for that comparison, before any route-specific rounding or minimum.

Now imagine a second packing proposal measuring 35 × 25 × 20 cm, with the same assumed actual weight. The calculated volumetric weight becomes approximately 2.92 kg. This shows why dimensions deserve attention. It does not prove the smaller box is feasible, sufficiently protective or cheaper under a real route's rules.

Record both proposals side by side. Ask whether the measurements describe a real packed parcel, whether delicate surfaces remain protected and whether original packaging is needed. Avoid treating removal of every box as an automatic saving. A decision that damages the item or removes packaging you wanted may defeat the purpose of the purchase.

Read minimum units and weight increments

The official calculator explains a first chargeable weight unit and subsequent units. This means the price may not rise smoothly gram by gram. In a hypothetical tariff with a 500 g first unit and later 100 g increments, crossing a boundary can affect the charged quantity. Use the actual route terms before doing any monetary calculation.

Save the first-unit rule, subsequent-unit rule and rounding instruction beside the estimate if they are shown. A comparison that records only the final number is difficult to reproduce later. Where the rule is unclear, leave the calculation unresolved rather than choosing a convenient rounding method.

Compare eligible routes on the same parcel

Compare routes using identical destination, contents, weight and dimensions. Changing one input between quotes makes it impossible to tell whether a price difference comes from the route or the parcel. Capture the date and any visible inclusion, restriction or size-limit notes with each result.

Route availability depends on the destination, and delivery services can have weight limits. Treat an unavailable or overweight combination as a constraint to resolve. Do not alter the declared contents to force a route to appear. If splitting a parcel is an option, estimate each resulting parcel separately because minimum charges may apply more than once.

Keep the estimate separate from payment

Use three distinct records: the early planning estimate, the shipping option displayed when the parcel is ready, and the amount actually paid or later shown in the account. This is an editorial bookkeeping method, not a claim that every order follows the same charging or adjustment procedure.

When the figures differ, compare the inputs first. Did packed weight change? Did the dimensions include protective material? Is the same route still selected? Does the currency match? Was an optional service added? An input-by-input comparison is more useful than assuming a difference represents an error or a guaranteed refund.

Run a sensitivity check before buying more items

For early budgeting, create a smaller, central and larger parcel scenario. Change only uncertain inputs such as packaging allowance or box height. Keep the known destination and intended contents fixed. Label each result as a scenario rather than displaying a single precise shipping promise.

Suppose the central scenario fits your budget but the larger-box scenario does not. That is a reason to resolve packing uncertainty or reduce the shortlist before placing another order. Adding a cheap product may still increase the delivered total if it changes the box size or creates another parcel.

Save a record that can be checked later

Your estimate record should include the parcel version, destination, item list, measurement source, units, route, displayed estimate, currency and observation date. Keep account-specific information private. If you share a screenshot, remove names, addresses and account identifiers before publishing it.

After shipping, retain the final dimensions and paid amount alongside the old estimates. The purpose is to learn which assumptions were weak, not to create a permanent rate chart. A past parcel can inform the next planning range, but its price and availability cannot guarantee the next shipment.

Continue from weight to a complete budget

Shipping is one part of a purchase. Keep item prices and seller-to-warehouse delivery separate from the international parcel estimate. Use the total-cost budget guide to avoid counting the same fee twice or leaving an unknown charge out of your decision.
